# Fix three errors before the Yellow Zone slide ships

The "Yellow Zone: The Beating Heart" slide is mostly accurate, but it has three errors and several claims nobody can verify. **The Garden Market flood was July 16, 2024, not 2023.** Evergreen's own 2024 materials name 2013 as the previous severe flood, so no damaging 2023 flood happened. **The café is not in the Welcome Centre.** Picnic Café is in Building 14. **No source mentions exposed timber in the TD Future Cities Centre.** Sources describe brick and steel. Three more claims need softer wording. The "$16M revitalization" is officially **$15.6M**, though "nearly $16 million" is Evergreen's own phrase. Evergreen never calls the Garden Market an "incubator." No source gives a figure for "thousands of dollars in inventory." The Koerner Gardens text checks out: part of it is copied almost word for word from Evergreen's page, and the free winter skating is confirmed. The slide also needs a timing update. A "100-year" storm flooded the site again on the night of **September 2, 2026**. The site closed, and the Saturday Farmers Market moved to the Distillery District for at least one weekend. By October 7, 2026, Evergreen listed the site as "Open daily." This was the third major flood in about a dozen years, after 2013 and 2024. That flood history is also the site's best teaching material for Urban Minds' 1UP students, who first visited the Brick Works at the 2018 Leaders Lab.

## Welcome Centre: a new front door, but not the café

The Welcome Centre was formerly the "Young Welcome Centre." It once hosted sustainable-living programs and exhibits ([Holcim Foundation](https://www.holcimfoundation.org/about/news/project-updates/evergreen-brick-works-heritage-site-revitalization-toronto-canad)). Today it is the first of five pieces in **Evergreen Brick Works 2.0, a $15.6M revitalization announced March 18, 2026**. The other four are the TD Future Cities Centre, the Children's Garden, the Ravine Centre and Tiffany Commons. Funding came from the Government of Canada, the Government of Ontario, the City of Toronto, the Ontario Trillium Foundation, and Tim and Frances Price. No amounts per funder were published ([Evergreen release](https://www.evergreen.ca/news/evergreen-brick-works-2-0-15-6m-revitalization-to-strengthen-climate-resilience-and-accessibility-of-historic-site/)). The Welcome Centre work adds:

- a new vestibule
- an accessible reception area
- a flexible program space
- a skylight
- seven self-contained washrooms
- flood-resilience upgrades
- the "Welcome Tower," with *The Promise*, a Corten-steel and stained-glass work by Ojibwa artist **Bonnie Devine**

The work was targeted to be "largely completed by Summer 2026" ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/evergreen-brick-works/revitalization/)). The design is by LGA Architectural Partners and DTAH, working as a joint venture ([UrbanToronto](https://urbantoronto.ca/news/2026/03/evergreen-brick-works-20-revitalization-advances-16m-investment.60627)). Toronto Life's "$16-million facelift" headline shows the rounding is common ([Toronto Life](https://torontolife.com/real-estate/evergreen-brick-works-revitalization-climate-restaurant-dtah/)).

Two gaps matter for the slide. No source confirms the Welcome Centre work was actually finished. No source reports whether the September 2026 flood damaged the new spaces. The slide should describe what the Welcome Centre contains, not claim it is complete.

The "café" error has a simple cause. Café Belong, the site's former restaurant, was replaced around January 2023 by Picnic Café in Building 14. Picnic closed temporarily for clean-up after the September 2 flood ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/?p=2833)). Calling the Welcome Centre "the heart" also conflicts with Evergreen's own language. Evergreen describes Tiffany Commons as "in many ways the Brick Works' front door" ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/evergreen-brick-work/activities/the-tiffany-commons/)). "Beating heart" works as a metaphor for the slide's zone as a whole, but should not be attributed to Evergreen.

**Suggested wording:** "Welcome Centre: the site's front door. It has visitor reception, new accessible washrooms, flexible program space and Bonnie Devine's *The Promise* in the new Welcome Tower. It is part of Evergreen Brick Works 2.0, a $15.6M (nearly $16M) revitalization announced in 2026. Picnic Café (Building 14), the Garden Market and the Saturday Farmers Market are a short walk away."

## Garden Market: wrong flood year, and an unsourced loss figure

The Evergreen Garden Market is Evergreen's own social-enterprise shop and garden centre. It sends "100% of its proceeds back to Evergreen" and sells native plants, compost, tools, seeds, and Christmas trees in season. It is open Monday to Sunday, with no seasonal closure listed ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/evergreen-brick-work/activities/evergreen-garden-market/)). Evergreen says "local, small businesses are the heart of the Evergreen Garden Market," and the shop carries goods from more than 100 local artisans and makers ([Evergreen Flood Recovery](https://www.evergreen.ca/flood-recovery/)).

The "incubator" label fits the separate **Saturday Farmers Market** better. That market was founded in 2007 by Elizabeth Harris with 25 vendors ([UTSC Feeding City](https://www.utsc.utoronto.ca/projects/feedingcity/2024/11/27/2907/)). It now hosts about 70 vendors outdoors and 50 indoors, recorded 108,754 visits in 2022, and moves indoors to the TD Future Cities Centre in winter. Evergreen explicitly argues that low-cost stalls help small businesses enter the market ([Evergreen Public Markets](https://www.evergreen.ca/impacts/public-markets/)). The slide should not blur the two markets.

The flood claim needs correcting. On **July 16, 2024**, about 100 mm of rain pushed the Don River over its banks. Staff, tenants and 113 campers were evacuated with no injuries ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/?p=27844)). Evergreen listed the Garden Market among the "hardest hit" areas. Its assistant manager, Michelle Zeppieri, said it "experienced significant damage, leading to considerable losses." Evergreen called the overall recovery "a multi-million dollar effort" and noted that insurers will not offer flood coverage on the floodplain ([Evergreen Flood Recovery](https://www.evergreen.ca/flood-recovery/)). The same materials say the previous severe flood was in **2013**, which rules out a damaging 2023 event.

No source gives a Garden Market dollar figure. "Devastated" is stronger than Evergreen's own words, though Evergreen staff did use "devastating" about other spaces. No source found describes damage to the Garden Market from the September 2, 2026 flood.

**Suggested wording:** "Evergreen Garden Market: a year-round social-enterprise shop and garden centre for gardeners and nature lovers. It showcases 100+ local artisans and small businesses, and 100% of proceeds support Evergreen. It suffered significant damage and considerable losses in the July 2024 flood, and the site flooded again in September 2026."

## TD Future Cities Centre: a kiln building, not timber

The Centre occupies **Building 16, the Tunnel Kiln and Dryer Building**. It was built in 1956–57 and is the largest heritage-designated structure on the campus ([UrbanToronto](https://urbantoronto.ca/news/2017/08/evergreen-brick-works-kiln-building-be-urban-innovation-hub)). The project team was:

- LGA Architectural Partners, lead architect
- ERA Architects, heritage
- EllisDon, construction

Work began in March 2017, and Phase 1 opened in May 2018. The 53,000 sq ft building is "fully enclosed for the first time" ([Canadian Architect](https://www.canadianarchitect.com/first-phase-completed-for-lgas-evergreen-kiln-building-redevelopment/)). The conservation approach was minimal: the kilns, the thin uninsulated walls and even rave-era graffiti were kept. Steel inlays trace the old kiln-car tracks in the new floor ([gb&d](https://gbdmagazine.com/evergreen-brick-works/)).

The climate claims hold up. A ground-source system with almost 50 boreholes is tied to **almost 300 solar thermal panels**. It feeds radiant in-floor heating and cooling, and the 2018 design was predicted to avoid 647 tonnes of carbon a year ([gb&d](https://gbdmagazine.com/evergreen-brick-works/)). The building also has:

- rooftop rainwater that supplies the toilets and irrigation
- a floor raised 2 ft on a cavity system for flood protection
- a retractable wall of high-performance windows

A 2024 federal grant of $2.4M expanded the rooftop solar thermal array, with an estimated 77.7% energy reduction ([Finance Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/department-finance/news/2024/08/deputy-prime-minister-announces-nearly-24-million-to-improve-torontos-evergreen-brick-works.html)).

The team considered LEED and Passive House and chose a **carbon-neutral target** instead. No certification exists, and no source confirms the target was met. The slide should say "designed to" rather than "proving." The Centre is the national hub of Future Cities Canada and hosts the Farmers Market in winter. That makes it the most climate-themed room on the site.

**Suggested wording:** "TD Future Cities Centre: the restored 1950s Kiln Building, the largest heritage structure on site, with its original tunnel kilns still in place. It is an event and exhibition venue, the hub of Future Cities Canada and the winter home of the Farmers Market. Geothermal heating and cooling, paired with solar thermal panels, rainwater reuse and a flood-raised floor, were designed to a carbon-neutral target. The building shows how heritage buildings can be adapted for a low-carbon future."

## Koerner Gardens: accurate, with a heritage setting worth adding

Evergreen's page calls Koerner Gardens a "hub for community events, recreational activities, public art projects and more" ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/evergreen-brick-work/activities/koerner-gardens/)), so the slide matches it almost word for word. What the slide leaves out is the setting. White pines grow up through the surviving roof girders of the old factory. A recent redesign with Trophic Design added:

- a lit, laser-engraved Corten-steel constellation art wall
- tiered stone seating
- native mound plantings

Beanfield supported the mound project, and Elder Catherine Tàmmaro guided it. It was completed around late 2023 or early 2024 ([Beanfield](https://blog.beanfield.com/?p=2450)).

In winter the gardens become an indoor-outdoor rink with an ECO CHILL system, running roughly December to early March. Public skating is free. Skate rental is a suggested $5 donation that "supports free programming," helmets are free, and lessons are paid ([Evergreen Skating](https://www.evergreen.ca/evergreen-brick-works/explore/recreation/skating/)). Skating runs at set holiday and weekend times, not daily. Dates for the 2026–27 season were not posted as of October 8 ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/evergreen-brick-work/activities/free-public-skate/)). No source explains the "Koerner" name, so leave it off the slide.

**Suggested wording:** "Koerner Gardens: a free, open public garden under the exposed beams of the old brick factory. It has native plantings, a Corten-steel constellation art wall, and space for community events, recreation and public art. In winter it becomes a skating rink with free public skating (skate rentals by suggested $5 donation)."

## Youth angles: three floods and a 2018 precedent

The site's history gives 1UP students a clear story. William Taylor found clay here in 1882. The Don Valley Brick Works opened in 1889 and supplied brick for Casa Loma and Massey Hall. It operated until 1984 ([Canadian Encyclopedia](https://development.thecanadianencyclopedia.ca/article/evergreen-brick-works)). After it closed, the City and TRCA expropriated the land, the quarry became a park in 1996, and 16 buildings were heritage-designated in 2002. Evergreen opened the site in **September 2010**. Some 2026 coverage says "2012," but that conflicts with Evergreen's own "fifteen years ago" phrasing. The site draws **more than 500,000 visitors a year** ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/evergreen-brick-works/visitor-info/about-us/)).

The site uses "wet floodproofing": water is allowed through the ground floors instead of being kept out. Features include:

- a Cupolex raised foundation
- mechanical systems on the second floor or higher
- no drywall or carpet on ground floors
- 20,000-litre cisterns
- pervious concrete
- stormwater greenways

These features worked "as designed" in 2013 ([Meeting of the Minds](https://meetingoftheminds.org/green-resilient-building-tested-at-evergreen-brick-works-8375)). Floods in 2024 and 2026 then exceeded what the design could absorb. On September 2, 2026, tenant Outward Bound Canada lost gear and a truck and expected no access for all of September ([Outward Bound](https://www.outwardbound.ca/blog/outward-bound-canada-toronto-flood-2026/)). CTV reported the weekend closure and the move of the Farmers Market ([CTV](https://www.ctvnews.ca/toronto/video/2026/09/05/evergreen-brick-works-closed-this-weekend-for-storm-cleanup-farmers-market-relocated/)). That gives students a live design problem: how to keep a heritage site and its small businesses running on a floodplain that insurers will not cover.

The link to Urban Minds is documented but historical. The **first 1UP Leaders Lab (August 17–19, 2018)** took 22 high school students to Evergreen Brick Works and ended with a Design Jam ([8 80 Cities](https://www.880cities.org/?p=4429)). No source shows a current formal partnership, so the slide should present it as precedent, not as an ongoing partnership.

Evergreen's own teen programming offers points of contact. It ran **more than 8,000 school-visit students and 3,736 bursaries in 2025–26**, and a new **Creating Our Climate Future** program for Grades 10–11 ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/stories/recapping-our-2025-2026-school-visits-at-evergreen-brick-works/)). It also offers an Outdoor Leadership Course and volunteer roles for ages up to 17 ([Evergreen](https://www.evergreen.ca/evergreen-brick-works/explore/children-and-youth/)). Each of the four sites suggests a 1UP prompt:

- **Welcome Centre:** accessible arrival and wayfinding.
- **Garden Market and Farmers Market:** small-business resilience after a flood.
- **TD Future Cities Centre:** heritage retrofit and carbon.
- **Koerner Gardens:** year-round public space and the free-rink model.

No public source uses the term "Yellow Zone" for the Brick Works. Treat it as the presentation's own label.

## Conclusion

Both main errors on the slide are recency errors. It cites a flood year that is wrong, and it places the café where the old café used to be. Both come from relying on secondhand descriptions of a site that has changed three times since 2023: it flooded in 2024, it was rebuilt in 2026, and it flooded again weeks after that rebuild. Any presentation given this fall should frame the Brick Works as an active test case rather than a finished showpiece. Its flood design is good enough to limit structural damage, but not to protect ground-floor inventory or programs.

For 1UP, that gap matters more than any amenity list. The "beating heart" is a heritage site that keeps adapting to climate stress, and that is a stronger city-building lesson than a café and a skating rink. Before the slide goes out, confirm two things directly with Evergreen: whether the Welcome Centre work is finished, and whether Picnic Café has reopened.
