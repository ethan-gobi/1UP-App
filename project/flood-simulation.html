    Solution: Flood-Ready Yellow Zone

    Know hours ahead, keep water out of the two key buildings, get stock off the floor, and reopen in under two weeks. The market and events keep running somewhere dry in the meantime. In the simulation it raises all four users from 8–23 points to 72–92, for about $0.5–0.9M.

    Part 1: The 3-legged stool

    1. Climate adaptation and nature-based infrastructure. The site already uses wet floodproofing: water is allowed through ground floors, and there is no drywall or carpet. We keep that. We add self-closing barriers only at the Garden Market and TD Future Cities Centre doors, so the rest of the floodplain still drains the way it does now. Rain gardens at Koerner Gardens handle everyday storms. We do not claim they stop a river flood. In the model they lower it by only a few centimetres.

    2. Heritage conservation. Nothing is fixed to historic brick. The barrier frames sit in new floor slabs at the doors, the shelving is movable furniture, and the status screen goes in the new Welcome Centre. Everything can be removed without a trace. That is why we rejected the dome, the underground tank and moving the buildings.

    3. Technology and sustainable energy. River-level sensors and the Don River gauge at Todmorden feed one Green / Yellow / Red status. It shows on a solar-powered screen, the website and opt-in texts. Yellow comes from the forecast, 6–12 hours ahead. Red comes from the gauge, 2–4 hours ahead. The sensors use very little power, which fits a site built around geothermal and solar thermal energy.

    Part 2: Hybrid solution architecture

    Physical intervention
    • Self-closing door barriers (0.9 m) at the Garden Market and Future Cities Centre. They rise with the water, so nobody has to be on site at 2 a.m.
    • Slot-in barrier at the Welcome Centre, put up at closing time on a Yellow day.
    • Raised rolling shelves, with valuable stock kept on the top shelf (team Orange).
    • Pack-into carts for vendors (team Pink).
    • 48-hour reopen kit (pumps, dehumidifiers, cleaning gear) stored on the Kiln Building's second floor.
    • Signs marking a family route out to high ground.
    Operational and digital system
    • Green / Yellow / Red status. Yellow means prep (shelves up, carts packed, slot-in barrier in). Red means close and nobody enters the valley.
    • Texts to staff, vendors, families, campers' parents and event clients.
    • An on-call crew with paid standby and trained volunteers (team Orange).
    • A reopen playbook that names who does what in hours 0–48 and sets a target day for each zone.
    • A pop-up plan: the market moves to the raised Kiln floor or a partner site, and events use a backup venue booked in advance.
    • A web page showing what is open today, plus the Kiln Building's second floor as the shelter point.

    Part 4: Full proposed solution write-up

    What. A low-cost package for the Yellow Zone. It combines a live flood status screen with text alerts, self-closing barriers at the two key doors, raised rolling shelves and pack-into carts for stock, a 48-hour reopen kit with an on-call crew, a pop-up plan for the market and events, and a signed family route with a shelter point.

    Why. In the 2024 and 2026 floods, the buildings did most of what they were designed to do. People were hurt in other ways: lost stock, about five weeks of closure, and no clear word on what was open. This package gives each user something they can see. Maya gets one source of truth and a playbook. Daniel can check the status before leaving home and knows where to go on site. Aisha keeps her stock and can sell from a pop-up by day 2. Marcus gets a dry venue and a backup he can promise his clients.

    How. A Yellow alert arrives before closing time. Staff put shelves up, pack the carts and put in the Welcome Centre barrier, then go home. Nobody is sent into the valley at night. The other barriers close by themselves. After the water drops, the crew pumps, washes and dries everything using the kit. In the model the site is open daily again around day 9, not day 35. Nothing touches heritage brick, and the cost is about $0.5–0.9M. A dome or relocation would cost tens of millions.

    Weak spots we admit. Koerner Gardens still floods and stays shut for about three weeks, so Daniel's family space and the rink area come back last. A storm bigger than the 0.9 m barriers gets in. Test it with "Bigger than ever": Marcus's venue floods and his score drops to about 59. Forecast-based Yellow alerts will sometimes close the site early for storms that miss. Barrier pits at the doors still need TRCA and heritage sign-off.
